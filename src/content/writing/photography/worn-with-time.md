When you walk up the stairs to the parapets of an ancient church, and gaze upon the stone blocks upon which so many have trodden. Do you not see the grinding that those feet have imparted? Is there not a dent in the stone that was clearly unintended from the perspective of the architect?

Billion-dollar businesses are based in the process of degradation, soliciting services that guarantee an object's ability to return to functioning ordering. A car mechanic may repair broken down cars for her whole career, a dentist, rotting teeth.

It is always interesting, to me, to look upon the objects that people have determined are not worthy of repair. Or perhaps the people themselves have been deemed so unworthy by the society in which they live, that they cannot afford to deem such a repair as worthy. Or, and perhaps most interestingly, when the disrepair of an object signifies age more so than a degradation of function, when it is deliberately left worn as testament to this age.

That last one is what we observe in [the tombstone below](#tombstone).

Even if there were someone alive who could recall what his face looked like, when he was alive, the wear of his features has made him completely unrecognizable. It is difficult to say, in fact, what position this man might have been in. There is clearly a dressing accompanying this pictorial, the bulbous hat, conical robes, staff, and glowing cranial backdrop (an artistic mind is required for this particular envisioning), and I would argue it bishop-like.

One facet of this rendering which has clearly been ground into nothingness is the pattern of the man's clothing. Near the raised staff, which would have protected its immediate, sunken robe background from the planar grinding surface created by many shoes, it can be observed that some sort of pattern is ingrained into the robe. The staff saved parts of the man's left shoulder, but his right was left undefended, and bears no witness to any chiseling of a pattern into the stone.
